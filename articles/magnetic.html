Earth's Magnetic Field: A Guide to the Magnetic Field Mapper

What a magnetometer really measures, why nearby metal ruins the reading, and how to interpret vector and anomaly data.

The Earth behaves like a weak, tilted bar magnet. Its field is always present, always changing slightly, and it points in a direction that varies with location, which makes it extraordinarily useful for navigation. It is also fragile in the hands of a phone. The magnetometer that detects it is easily overwhelmed by the steel in a desk, the speaker in a laptop or the battery in the device itself.

The Field Instruments Magnetic Field Mapper reads the three-axis magnetic field and presents it as a vector, a magnitude, a trend and an anomaly view. This guide explains the physics, the practical calibration steps, and the honest limits of a consumer sensor.

Earth's magnetic field

The geomagnetic field is generated by motion in the Earth's liquid outer core, a process called the geodynamo. At the surface the field is not uniform. Total intensity ranges roughly from twenty-five to sixty-five microtesla depending on latitude, with the strongest values near the magnetic poles. The field has both a horizontal and a vertical component. The angle between the field and the horizontal is called inclination; the angle between magnetic north and true north is called declination.

Declination is why a magnetic compass does not point to the geographic pole. It varies by location and changes slowly over time, so navigation systems carry a declination model. The mapper reports the raw vector, which you can resolve into components if you need to reason about heading separately from dip.

Earth's field also fluctuates. Solar activity, magnetic storms and local geological features all perturb it. These variations are small on a normal day but can be measurably different between quiet and disturbed conditions.

How the magnetometer works

Most consumer magnetometers use a Hall-effect or magnetoresistive element whose electrical properties change in the presence of a magnetic field. Three sensors on orthogonal axes measure the field vector, and the software combines them into a magnitude and direction. The sensor reports in microtesla, with each axis describing the component in its own direction.

Because the device also contains permanent magnets in its speaker and sometimes in its camera assembly, the raw readings include a fixed offset from the device itself. This is a hard-iron offset. In addition, nearby ferromagnetic objects distort the field lines and create a soft-iron distortion. Together these effects mean that the magnitude you see can differ from the true geomagnetic field unless the sensor is calibrated.

Distortion and calibration

A standard calibration technique is the figure-eight motion. Rotating the device slowly through all orientations traces the magnetic vector on the surface of a sphere in a clean environment. The offset shifts the sphere's centre away from the origin, and the shape may be stretched into an ellipsoid. Calibration estimates that centre and the stretch so that the software can recover a corrected vector.

Even with calibration, environment matters more than most users expect. Steel furniture, reinforced concrete, cars, elevators, headphones and even a watch can dominate the local field. If you want to measure the Earth's field, move away from these objects, hold the device away from your body and metal, and repeat a measurement in several places to see which values are stable.

Reading the output

The vector readout gives the field along each axis. Its direction is only meaningful relative to the device orientation, so rotate the device deliberately and watch how the components trade off. The magnitude is the length of the vector and is the most stable single number, but remember that it includes the device's own offset unless calibration has been applied.

The trend and history views show how the field changes over time. A sudden step change usually means you moved near a ferromagnetic object rather than that the Earth's field changed; a slow drift may be device or temperature related. The anomaly view highlights deviations from a baseline, which is useful for mapping how a room or object perturbs the field.

When reading anomaly data, establish a baseline in a clean location and then map around the object of interest. The pattern of increases and decreases can reveal hidden metal, the orientation of a magnet or the shape of a field source. This is a kind of qualitative magnetometry, effective for education and exploration but not for survey-grade mapping.

Practical applications

Navigation is the original application. A magnetometer provides a heading that is independent of GPS and works when satellites are unavailable, though declination and calibration must be handled. Education is the second obvious use: the mapper makes an invisible field tangible and shows why compasses behave as they do.

Surveyors and geophysicists use far more sensitive instruments to map geology, archaeology and buried structures. A phone cannot replace them, but it can introduce the concepts. Electronics troubleshooting can also benefit; the mapper can show which side of a device is producing a strong field. Hobbyists use it to find studs, check magnet orientation and explore sensor behaviour.

Limitations and accuracy

Consumer magnetometers are designed for compasses, not for scientific measurement. Their resolution, linearity and noise characteristics are modest, and the device's own hard-iron offset is significant. Nearby ferromagnetic material can produce errors larger than the field being studied. Temperature and battery state can shift the offset over time.

For reliable results, calibrate in a clean environment, avoid metal, keep the device orientation stable during a reading, and compare multiple locations. Report trends and relative differences rather than absolute values. For applications that require calibrated magnetometry, use a dedicated instrument with a known specification.
